What Happened

Uber Technologies used its second-quarter 2026 earnings call, held August 6, to formalize what has become its core AI strategy: stop trying to build self-driving cars itself and instead become the dispatch, demand, and data layer that every autonomous vehicle (AV) developer plugs into. CEO Dara Khosrowshahi told analysts that Uber now has commercial or piloted partnerships with over a dozen AV operators — including Waymo, Nvidia (via its DRIVE Hyperion platform), Wayve, WeRide, Pony.ai, Momenta, Avride, and Nuro — spanning more than 20 metro areas across the U.S., Middle East, and Europe.

The headline number Uber highlighted was cumulative autonomous trips booked through its app, which it said crossed a multi-million-ride threshold since the Waymo partnership began in 2023, with AV rides now a low-single-digit but fast-growing share of total U.S. gross bookings in cities where the service is live, such as Phoenix, Austin, and Atlanta.

Separately, Uber disclosed that its AI Solutions unit — the data-labeling and human-feedback business it built out of what was formerly called Uber Scaled Solutions — is now on a run rate exceeding $300 million annually, up sharply from prior year, as AI labs shop for RLHF and annotation vendors outside of Scale AI.

💡
What "AI Solutions" actually sells: Uber repurposes its existing gig workforce — over 7 million drivers and couriers globally — to do contract work for AI labs: labeling images, rating chatbot outputs, and testing agents, billed per task much like a ride.

Why It Matters

Uber's bet is structural, not technical: rather than pick a winning AV stack, it collects a toll on whichever one wins by owning the rider-facing app and the demand data that AV fleets need to route efficiently. That's a meaningfully different posture than Tesla's Robotaxi/Cybercab push or Amazon's Zoox, both of which are vertically integrated and don't need — or want — a third-party dispatcher.

The winners here are the AV startups themselves. Companies like Wayve and Avride get instant rider demand without having to build consumer apps, marketing, or payments infrastructure from scratch — a huge cost and time savings. Uber wins by keeping its take rate on rides it never has to pay a human driver for, which is structurally more profitable at scale. The clearer loser, longer term, is the traditional gig driver base Uber built its business on; Uber has been careful in public messaging to frame AVs as "supplementing" driver supply during peak demand, not replacing it, but the economics point toward gradual displacement in dense urban corridors first.

Lyft, which has far fewer AV partnerships (mainly May Mobility and a smaller Waymo pilot in Nashville), is increasingly exposed if AV-capable riders start defaulting to Uber in shared markets.

Context

This continues a pattern set in motion at CES in January 2025, when Uber and Nvidia announced a joint plan to help automakers and AV developers build toward a fleet target of up to 100,000 Level 4-capable vehicles using Nvidia's DRIVE Hyperion computer and safety stack. Uber has since layered on additional bilateral deals rather than picking one partner exclusively — a hedging strategy distinct from Waymo, which builds and operates its own fleet end-to-end in cities like San Francisco and Los Angeles without Uber.

The AI Solutions growth also has a competitive backstory: after Meta took a roughly 49% stake in Scale AI and hired away CEO Alexandr Wang in mid-2025, several major AI labs began diversifying their data-labeling vendors to avoid depending on a supplier partly owned by a rival lab. Uber, along with Surge AI and Mercor, was a direct beneficiary of that reshuffling — turning Uber's idle driver-hours into a second AI-adjacent revenue stream.

💡
Key metric to track: Uber's AV "trip mix" by city, disclosed quarterly. Once AV rides pass roughly 10% of trips in a given metro, expect visible pricing and driver-supply effects in that market specifically — not nationally.

What To Do Next

  • Riders in Phoenix, Austin, Atlanta, and Abu Dhabi can already select AV rides in-app when available; expect gradual city rollout rather than a national switch.
  • AV developers evaluating go-to-market options should weigh Uber's distribution against the margin cost of its take rate versus building direct consumer demand, as Waymo has chosen to do.
  • AI teams sourcing human feedback or annotation work now have a credible third option beyond Scale AI and Surge AI in Uber AI Solutions, worth a vendor comparison on cost and turnaround for RLHF-style tasks.
  • Investors should watch Uber's next two quarterly reports for whether AI Solutions revenue disclosure becomes a standalone segment — that would signal Uber sees it as a durable pillar, not a side hustle.
  • Lyft and other ride-hailing competitors without comparable AV partnership breadth face rising pressure to either strike similar multi-partner deals or cede AV-heavy metros to Uber outright.
