What Happened

A data breach is any incident where sensitive, protected, or confidential information is accessed, copied, transmitted, or stolen by someone who isn't authorized to have it. That covers a wide range of scenarios: an attacker exploiting a software vulnerability, an employee misconfiguring a cloud storage bucket, a phishing email that hands over login credentials, or a third-party vendor getting hacked and exposing data it was holding on your behalf.

The scale of the problem has grown sharply in recent years. IBM's annual Cost of a Data Breach report put the global average cost of a breach at $4.88 million in 2024, up from $4.45 million the year before — the largest jump since the pandemic. Some of the largest recent incidents illustrate how varied breaches can be: the 2023 MOVEit file-transfer exploit compromised data at more than 2,600 organizations worldwide through a single vulnerable vendor product; the February 2024 Change Healthcare ransomware attack disrupted prescription processing nationwide and ultimately exposed records tied to roughly 100 million people; and the 2023 23andMe breach, caused by credential-stuffing rather than a direct hack, exposed genetic and ancestry data for 6.9 million users.

What ties these together — and why an AI industry outlet is explaining the term now — is that the attack surface for breaches has expanded as companies plug AI tools into systems that hold customer data, source code, and internal documents. An AI copilot with broad read access to a company's file storage, or an autonomous agent with API keys to production systems, is a new kind of target that didn't exist five years ago.

💡
Quick check: You can see whether your own email or credentials have shown up in a known breach using free lookup services like Have I Been Pwned. If a service you use appears there, rotate the password immediately and turn on multi-factor authentication.

Why It Matters

Breaches are no longer just an IT problem — they're a board-level and product-design problem, especially for companies building AI features.

  • Bigger blast radius with AI agents. An AI agent with standing access to email, CRM, or codebases can be tricked or hijacked into exfiltrating far more data, far faster, than a human insider ever could.
  • Training data is now a liability surface. If customer data is used to fine-tune or embed into a model, a breach isn't just about stolen files anymore — it raises questions about whether that data can be extracted back out of the model itself.
  • Security vendors are the clear winners. Google's roughly $32 billion acquisition of cloud security startup Wiz, announced in 2025, is the clearest signal that data and cloud security has become one of the most valuable categories in tech, not a cost center.
  • Regulators are moving faster than before. The SEC's cyber disclosure rule, in effect since December 2023, requires public companies to disclose material breaches within four business days — a much tighter window than the weeks or months companies used to take.

Context

Data breaches aren't new, but the industry's response to them has shifted meaningfully over the past three years. Security spending has moved away from pure network-perimeter defense (firewalls, VPNs) toward data-centric security — knowing where sensitive data actually lives, who and what can touch it, and flagging when access patterns look abnormal. This category, often called DSPM (data security posture management), barely existed as a labeled market segment before 2022 and is now one of the fastest-growing corners of cybersecurity spending.

At the same time, a new subcategory of AI-specific risk has emerged alongside classic breaches: prompt injection attacks that trick an AI agent into leaking data it has access to, and model-extraction attacks that try to reconstruct training data through repeated queries. These aren't hypothetical — security researchers have demonstrated both against production systems, which is part of why enterprise buyers now ask AI vendors pointed questions about data isolation before signing contracts.

The Change Healthcare incident is also reshaping how the industry thinks about vendor concentration risk: because a huge share of U.S. prescription and claims processing ran through one company, a single breach cascaded into a nationwide operational crisis. That's the same structural risk regulators and buyers now worry about with AI infrastructure — a handful of foundation-model providers and cloud platforms sit underneath an enormous share of AI products.

💡
For context: Under the SEC's rule, "material" breaches must be disclosed in an 8-K filing within four business days of a company determining the incident is material — not four days from discovery, which is a distinction that has already caused disputes over timing.

What To Do Next

For developers and teams shipping AI features, the practical steps are concrete rather than exotic:

  • Apply least privilege to AI agents. Don't grant a chatbot or agent broader read/write access than the specific task requires, and scope API keys narrowly instead of reusing admin credentials.
  • Audit third-party and subprocessor access. Most recent large breaches (MOVEit, Change Healthcare) started with a vendor, not the primary company — know what data your vendors and AI providers can see.
  • Encrypt data at rest and in transit, and use a secrets manager rather than hardcoding API keys or credentials in code or config files.
  • Have a disclosure plan ready before an incident happens, given the SEC's four-business-day clock.

For buyers evaluating AI vendors, ask directly: Where is our data stored, is it used for model training, what's the data retention period, and can you provide a current SOC 2 or ISO 27001 report? For individual users, the basics still matter most: unique passwords or passkeys per service, multi-factor authentication turned on everywhere it's offered, and periodic checks of breach-notification services so you're not the last to know your data is already out there.
