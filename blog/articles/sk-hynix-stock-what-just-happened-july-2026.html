What Happened

SK Hynix shares have been among the most-watched tickers on the KOSPI through the summer of 2026, as the company's second-quarter earnings reinforced its position as the primary memory supplier for Nvidia's AI accelerator roadmap. The stock has traded near record territory for much of the year, extending a rally that began in 2023 when the industry-wide shift to high-bandwidth memory (HBM) turned SK Hynix from a cyclical also-ran into the most profitable business unit inside the AI supply chain.

The latest earnings cycle centers on three things: continued sequential growth in operating profit driven almost entirely by HBM sales, confirmation that SK Hynix remains Nvidia's lead supplier for HBM4 modules feeding the Blackwell Ultra and upcoming Rubin platforms, and guidance that HBM output for 2026 is effectively sold out under multi-year supply agreements. That combination has kept analyst price targets climbing even as broader semiconductor indices have been choppy.

  • Earnings: Q2 2026 operating profit marked another consecutive quarterly record, with management citing HBM as the majority contributor to both revenue and margin for the first time in company history.
  • Product transition: SK Hynix confirmed it is shipping HBM4 samples and ramping volume production ahead of rivals, a generation that requires custom base-die logic co-designed with each GPU customer.
  • Customer concentration: Nvidia remains the dominant buyer, meaning SK Hynix's results now move in close correlation with Nvidia's own data center GPU shipment cadence.
💡
Quick fact check: HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) is a stacked DRAM architecture that sits directly next to a GPU die, giving AI accelerators the memory bandwidth they need to keep thousands of compute cores fed. It is not a new invention — the JEDEC standard dates back to 2013 — but AI training and inference workloads turned it from a niche graphics-card component into the tightest bottleneck in the entire AI hardware stack.

Why It Matters

SK Hynix's results are a proxy for the health of the entire AI infrastructure build-out. When HBM allocation is tight and priced at a premium, it tells you Nvidia (and by extension, hyperscalers like Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta) are still capacity-constrained on GPU output — not demand. That's a bullish signal for the AI capex cycle broadly, but it is a mixed one for everyone downstream of memory pricing.

Winners are fairly clear: SK Hynix itself, its equipment suppliers, and Nvidia, whose margins depend on securing enough HBM to ship complete GPU systems on schedule. Samsung Electronics, the world's largest memory maker by overall revenue, has been the visible loser in this specific race — it has struggled to pass Nvidia's qualification tests for HBM3E and HBM4 as quickly as SK Hynix, ceding the highest-margin slice of the memory market to a company it has historically outsized in DRAM overall. Micron Technology sits in between, qualified as a secondary HBM supplier but without SK Hynix's volume or lead-customer relationship.

The losers further downstream are PC makers, smartphone brands, and server OEMs outside the AI segment. Standard DRAM and NAND capacity has been squeezed as fabs prioritize wafer allocation toward HBM production, which uses more silicon per bit than conventional memory. That has pushed up contract prices for commodity DRAM used in laptops, phones, and consumer electronics — a knock-on cost that consumer hardware companies are now passing to buyers.

Context

This is the latest chapter in a run that started in 2023, when Nvidia's H100 shortage exposed HBM as the industry's real constraint rather than GPU logic itself. SK Hynix was first to mass-produce HBM3, then HBM3E, and locked in Nvidia as an anchor customer well before Samsung cleared qualification — a lead that compounded through 2024 and 2025 as SK Hynix reported record profits quarter after quarter while Samsung's memory division underperformed its own historical benchmarks.

The HBM4 transition now underway is more disruptive than prior generational shifts because it requires a custom logic base die tailored to each GPU vendor, effectively turning memory suppliers into co-design partners rather than commodity vendors. That raises switching costs for GPU makers and deepens the moat for whichever memory maker ships first and cleanest — which is precisely why SK Hynix's early HBM4 lead matters more than a typical one-quarter product edge.

💡
Watch the base die, not just the stack: In HBM4, the logic base die can be built on a customer's own foundry process rather than the memory maker's. Nvidia has reportedly pushed suppliers toward TSMC-fabricated base dies — a detail that matters because it ties HBM supply even more tightly to TSMC's advanced-node capacity, adding a second potential bottleneck behind memory itself.

What To Do Next

For investors, the SK Hynix story is now a leveraged bet on Nvidia's data center shipment volumes rather than a standalone memory-cycle trade — model it accordingly, and watch Nvidia's own GPU guidance as the leading indicator rather than SK Hynix's headline earnings alone.

  • AI infrastructure buyers: Expect continued lead times and premium pricing on HBM-equipped GPU systems through the rest of 2026; lock in allocation with cloud providers or OEMs early rather than waiting for spot availability.
  • Hardware and device makers: Budget for elevated commodity DRAM and NAND contract prices, since fab capacity is being reallocated toward HBM; consider longer-term supply contracts with Micron or Samsung, both of which have more spare non-HBM capacity than SK Hynix.
  • Competitors and challengers: Samsung's and Micron's HBM4 qualification timelines are the key variable to track — any successful Nvidia qualification for a second HBM4 source would ease pricing pressure and reduce SK Hynix's premium valuation.
  • Developers building on AI infrastructure: Memory scarcity, not just GPU compute scarcity, is shaping which cloud regions and instance types have availability — factor HBM supply news into capacity planning for large training runs.
