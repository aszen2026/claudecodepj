What Happened

The "xAI IPO" is finally here — just not under xAI's own name. SpaceX, which absorbed Elon Musk's AI lab in an all-stock merger completed in February 2026, priced its initial public offering on Thursday at $135 per share, selling 555,555,555 shares to raise roughly $75 billion. That makes it the largest IPO in history by a wide margin, blowing past Saudi Aramco's $29.4 billion record from 2019. Shares begin trading today, June 12, under the ticker SPCX.

The numbers behind the structure matter. The February merger valued xAI at about $250 billion as a wholly owned subsidiary inside a combined entity pegged at roughly $1.25 trillion. A standalone xAI listing is off the table; public-market exposure to Grok, the Colossus compute build-out, and the X platform now comes exclusively through SPCX stock.

The listing arrives with an asterisk. On June 10 — two days before trading opened — former xAI engineer Devin Kim filed suit in California state court against both xAI and SpaceX. Kim, who joined in 2024 and worked on Grok post-training before leaving in September 2025, alleges he was fired in retaliation for repeatedly raising safety concerns, including Grok's potential to foment discrimination and to spread information about weapons of mass destruction. The complaint alleges that xAI co-founder Jimmy Ba dismissed safety directives, told Kim "AI will kill us all anyway," and misrepresented aspects of Grok Code 1 to dodge legally required EU safety testing at release. xAI has not commented. Kim, notably, was named president of the nonprofit Center for AI Safety last week.

💡
Don't confuse the $75 billion raised with the company's worth. That figure is IPO proceeds — cash from shares sold. The combined SpaceX–xAI entity was valued near $1.25 trillion at the merger, and today's trading will set the first real public-market price.

Why It Matters

This is the first time public-market investors can buy meaningful exposure to a frontier AI lab. Until now, stakes in xAI, OpenAI, and Anthropic lived inside venture rounds and secondary sales accessible only to institutions and the well-connected. SPCX changes that — and the price the market assigns to the xAI portion of the business becomes a reference point for every private AI valuation that follows.

The winners are clear: early SpaceX and xAI investors get a liquidity event of unprecedented scale, and Musk consolidates rockets, satellites, compute, and a social platform into a single public vehicle with himself at the center. The risk side is equally concrete. Going public converts xAI's safety practices from an internal culture question into a securities-law question. If the allegation that the company misrepresented a model to evade EU testing requirements were ever substantiated, that is no longer just a regulatory problem — for a public company, misstatements about compliance can become disclosure and liability problems for shareholders.

The timing of Kim's lawsuit guarantees that AI safety governance is now a line item in analyst coverage of SPCX, not a niche policy debate.

Context

The deal caps an aggressive consolidation run. xAI acquired X (formerly Twitter) in March 2025, folding the social platform's data and distribution into the AI lab. The February 2026 SpaceX merger then stacked that combination under the aerospace company — the largest private corporate merger on record — before sprinting to a public listing within four months.

The competitive backdrop explains the urgency. CNN reported this week that OpenAI is widely expected to pursue its own listing, and Wall Street has spent the spring handicapping which frontier lab reaches public markets first. Musk got there before Sam Altman did, and the SPCX debut will be read as a referendum on how public investors price AI labs generally — including ones still private.

Kim's suit also fits a recognizable pattern: AI safety staff going public after leaving frontier labs, from the OpenAI departures and "right to warn" letter of 2024 onward. What's new is the venue. Those disputes used to play out in open letters and press interviews; this one is a court filing against a company that became publicly traded the same week.

What To Do Next

  • Investors: Read the risk-factor section of the SpaceX prospectus before buying, specifically the litigation and AI-regulation disclosures. Expect heavy first-week volatility, and note the lockup expiration date — insider supply hitting the market months from now is a known pressure point for mega-IPOs.
  • Developers building on Grok: A public-company quarterly cadence tends to change API economics. Watch for pricing changes and model deprecation schedules, and if you deploy Grok Code models for EU users, track how the regulatory testing allegations resolve — compliance status of the models you ship is now your problem too.
  • Enterprise buyers: Use this moment to ask all AI vendors — not just xAI — for written safety-testing and EU AI Act compliance documentation. The Kim complaint is a template for the questions procurement teams should already be asking.
💡
If you hold SPCX or plan to, set a calendar reminder for the first quarterly earnings report. It will contain the first audited, legally binding public disclosures xAI has ever made about its business — including how it characterizes the Kim litigation.
