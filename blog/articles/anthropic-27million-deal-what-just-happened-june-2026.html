What Happened

The most expensive fight in AI politics so far just ended without a winner. The roughly $27 million proxy war over New York Assemblymember Alex Bores — a contest that pitted the AI industry's biggest spenders against each other — closed in a draw, according to reporting today, June 25, 2026.

Bores is not an obscure figure. He authored New York's RAISE Act, one of the first state-level AI safety laws aimed at frontier model developers, and then ran for Congress. That combination made him a target. A pro-industry super PAC, Leading the Future — backed by names associated with a16z and OpenAI — had publicly signaled it would spend heavily against candidates friendly to AI regulation. Bores fit the profile exactly.

The headline numbers:

  • ~$27 million in combined spending flowed through the race, making it a marquee test of whether AI money could swing an election.
  • Anthropic sat on the opposite side of the deregulation-first PAC, aligning with its long-standing public position favoring guardrails on frontier models.
  • The result was a draw — neither the pro-regulation nor the deregulation camp walked away with a clean, decisive win.

In plain terms: two factions of the AI industry spent eight figures fighting each other over one politician, and the scoreboard came back even.

Why It Matters

The split is the story. This was not "tech versus its critics." It was Anthropic-aligned money against OpenAI- and a16z-aligned money. The industry that likes to present a united front on Capitol Hill just spent $27 million publicly disagreeing with itself about how much regulation it can live with.

That has real consequences:

  • The "AI lobby" is now two lobbies. Anthropic has staked out a safety-and-rules posture; the deregulation PAC wants the opposite. Lawmakers can no longer treat "what does the AI industry want" as a single answer.
  • A draw is a warning, not a victory. $27 million did not buy a decisive outcome. For the deregulation camp, that undercuts the threat that backing AI rules ends a political career. For the safety camp, it proves they can hold ground — but not that they can win outright.
  • State-level AI law survived the test. Bores became a target because the RAISE Act worked as a template. Knocking him out cleanly would have been a deterrent to other state legislators. The draw removes that deterrent.
💡
The signal for policymakers: when one frontier lab funds your defense and another funds your defeat, "the industry's position" is no longer a usable shorthand. Ask which company, and why.

Context

This fight did not come out of nowhere. Anthropic has spent the past two years differentiating itself on policy — supporting disclosure and safety requirements that several competitors have argued against. The contrast hardened as state legislatures, led by California and New York, moved on AI bills faster than Congress did.

Leading the Future formed explicitly to push back on that wave at the ballot box, modeled on the crypto industry's earlier, well-funded super PAC playbook — the one that did reshape several congressional races. The Bores contest was the first high-profile attempt to run that same play against an AI-safety candidate.

The crypto comparison is the one to watch. Crypto money largely worked: it cleared opponents and disciplined the field. AI money, on its first marquee outing, produced a tie. That gap matters. It suggests the deregulation PAC's spending does not yet carry the same scare factor — partly because, this time, there was serious money on the other side answering it.

What To Do Next

The takeaways depend on where you sit.

  • For developers and AI buyers: treat vendor regulatory posture as a procurement variable, not a press-release detail. Anthropic's compliance-forward stance and a deregulation-first competitor's stance imply different risk profiles for what they'll build, log, and disclose. Read each lab's published policy positions before you standardize on one.
  • For teams in regulated sectors: the RAISE Act surviving this fight means state-level AI obligations are not going away. If you operate in New York or California, map your models and data flows against existing state requirements now rather than waiting for a federal standard that this draw makes less likely to arrive soon.
  • For anyone tracking the industry: follow the money, not the mission statements. The clearest data on what each AI company actually wants is now in PAC filings, not in keynote talks.
💡
Concrete next step: bookmark the disclosure filings for Leading the Future and its opposing committees. The next race they target will show whether $27 million-for-a-draw was a one-off or the new baseline cost of AI politics.
