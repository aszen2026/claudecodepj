What Happened

Google is putting $75 million into A24, the independent studio behind Everything Everywhere All at Once, Hereditary, and Ex Machina. Alongside the investment, Google DeepMind is opening a dedicated AI filmmaking research partnership with the studio. The announcement landed June 23, 2026.

The two pieces are distinct, and worth separating before anyone collapses them into "Google bought its way into Hollywood":

  • The money: a $75M equity investment from Google into A24 — a stake, not an acquisition.
  • The research: a DeepMind partnership focused on AI tools for filmmaking. This is the part that signals where it's actually headed.

DeepMind already ships the relevant technology. Its Veo family of video-generation models is the lab's flagship in this space, and a research partnership with a working studio gives DeepMind something it can't buy outright: real directors, real productions, and real constraints to test against. A24, in turn, gets capital and early access to tooling.

💡
Read the structure, not the headline. A $75M check buys a stake; a research partnership buys feedback loops. The second is the strategically interesting half — it's how DeepMind turns a generative video model into something filmmakers will actually use on set.

Why It Matters

A24's whole brand is human authorship — auteur directors, distinctive scripts, films that feel made by someone. That makes it the most credible possible partner for AI tooling, and the riskiest. If A24 directors use DeepMind tools and the films still feel like A24 films, that's the strongest argument yet that generative AI can sit inside a serious creative pipeline without flattening it.

Who gains:

  • Google DeepMind gets a marquee creative validator for Veo and a counter-narrative to "AI slop." Adobe, Runway, and OpenAI are all chasing the same filmmaker audience; a studio relationship is hard to replicate.
  • A24 gets growth capital and a technology edge while keeping its independence — a minority stake, not a takeover.

Who should be nervous:

  • VFX houses and pre-viz shops, if generative tools absorb iteration work like previz, rotoscoping, and background plates.
  • Rival AI-video vendors, who now have to answer "which prestige studio is using you in production?"
  • Crew and craft guilds, who will read any "AI in filmmaking" headline through the lens of the 2023 WGA and SAG-AFTRA strikes that put AI guardrails into union contracts.

Context

This doesn't come from nowhere. Generative video crossed from novelty to usable craft over the past two years. OpenAI's Sora put text-to-video on the map, Runway pushed its Gen-series models into actual editing rooms — including a 2024 deal with Lionsgate to train a custom model on the studio's catalog — and DeepMind's Veo closed much of the quality gap while plugging into Google's distribution.

What's different here is the partner profile. Lionsgate is a catalog-heavy commercial studio, and that deal was largely about licensing a back catalog to train on. A24 is a tastemaker known for directorial vision and tight budgets, not franchise volume. DeepMind picking A24 is a bet that the credibility of how films get made matters more than the size of the library you train on. It also fits Google's broader pattern of pairing frontier models with domain partners who supply the messy real-world constraints a benchmark never captures.

💡
The benchmark that matters isn't clip length. It's whether a director can hold the same character, lighting, and tone across a sequence of shots. That production-grade consistency problem is what A24's involvement is meant to pressure-test — and the one rivals' demos still dodge.

What To Do Next

If you're a filmmaker or studio operator:

  • Treat Veo and its competitors as pre-production and iteration tools first — storyboards, mood films, VFX previz — where mistakes are cheap and nothing ships to an audience yet.
  • Get your rights and consent terms in writing before you generate anything featuring a performer's likeness or voice. The union guardrails from the 2023 strikes still apply, deal or no deal.

If you're a developer building on generative video:

  • Assume the bar just moved from "looks impressive in a demo" to "survives a director's notes." Build for controllability — shot consistency, character persistence, clean exports into existing editing pipelines.
  • Watch whether DeepMind productizes what it builds with A24. If a "Veo for production" tier appears, this collaboration is your spec sheet for what's actually supported.

If you're a buyer evaluating AI-video vendors:

  • Don't pay for headline clip quality. Ask vendors to demo a multi-shot sequence with a fixed character and setting — the exact thing this partnership is built to solve and the exact thing most demos avoid.
  • Ask the legitimacy question directly: who is using this in real production, and on what rights and indemnity terms? A $75M studio tie-up resets the reference customer everyone else gets compared against.

And for everyone watching the labor side: the number to track isn't $75 million. It's whether the first A24 project to use these tools credits the people who used them, and on what terms. That's the precedent this deal actually sets.
