What Actually Changed in AI Research This Year

Two years ago, "AI research tool" meant a chatbot that could summarize a PDF you pasted into it. In 2026, the category has split into three genuinely different species, and picking the wrong one wastes hours: fast-answer engines (sub-10-second, cited, shallow), agentic deep-research runners (5–30 minutes, dozens of sources, report-shaped output), and corpus-native tools that live inside a fixed body of documents you control.

The dividing line that matters is retrieval depth versus latency. A fast-answer engine typically pulls 5–15 pages. A deep-research agent will iterate: search, read, notice a gap, search again. In our testing, a single Gemini Deep Research run on a mid-complexity question ("how have EU AI Act GPAI obligations been enforced since August 2025?") touched 187 URLs and took 11 minutes. Perplexity answered the same question in 8 seconds off 9 sources — and got the enforcement timeline subtly wrong, because the correction lived in a follow-up filing that the shallow pass never reached.

Neither is "better." They're different tools, and the professionals getting real value are the ones who stopped trying to make one tool do both jobs.

The Contenders, Honestly Assessed

Deep-research agents

OpenAI Deep Research remains the strongest general-purpose long-horizon researcher, particularly for commercial and market questions where sources are scattered across press releases, filings, and trade press. Its weakness is confident synthesis: it will smooth over contradictions between sources rather than flag them. Read its citations, not just its prose.

Gemini Deep Research wins on raw breadth and on anything where Google's index has an edge — regional news, obscure PDFs, government portals. It's also the most transparent about its process, showing you the research plan before it runs. That plan is editable, and editing it is the single highest-leverage thing most users never do.

Claude with extended web search and Projects is the one I reach for when the research feeds directly into something I have to build or defend. It's noticeably more willing to say "the sources disagree" or "I couldn't find support for this," which is annoying in a demo and invaluable in a deliverable.

Academic and scientific search

Elicit and Consensus are not general research tools and shouldn't be judged as such. Elicit's real feature is structured extraction: give it 200 papers and a column schema ("sample size," "intervention," "effect direction"), and it builds the table you'd otherwise spend a week assembling. Undermind takes the opposite bet — slow, iterative, semantically driven search over roughly 250M papers, optimized for recall rather than speed. If your worry is "what am I missing," it's the best answer on the market.

Corpus-native

NotebookLM is still the most underrated tool in the category, precisely because it refuses to search the open web. Everything it says is grounded in the 50–300 sources you gave it. For due diligence, literature you've already curated, or internal documentation, that constraint is the feature.

💡
The citation-click test. Before trusting any AI research output, open three citations at random and check that the source actually says what the summary claims. Across informal testing of the major tools, roughly 1 in 8 citations is directionally right but overstated — the source hedges, the summary doesn't. Tools that hallucinate outright URLs are now rare; tools that over-read a real source are still everywhere.

If You're Building, Not Just Reading

The most consequential shift of 2026 isn't a chat product at all — it's that deep research became an API primitive. Exa's neural search, Anthropic's and OpenAI's web-search tools, and the growing pile of MCP servers mean you can now assemble a research agent yourself: fan out queries, fetch, verify claims adversarially, synthesize with citations.

Three things separate a research pipeline that works from one that produces plausible garbage:

  • Iterative retrieval beats one big search. One query → read → identify gaps → re-query. A single retrieval pass, no matter how many results it returns, systematically misses anything you didn't already know to ask about.
  • Verify claims separately from generating them. Have a second model attempt to refute each claim against its source. Anything the refuter kills was never a finding.
  • Cache aggressively. Fetching and re-reading the same 30 pages across iterations is where the token budget silently dies. Content-hash your fetches.
💡
Cost reality check. A serious agentic research run — 40+ pages fetched, claims verified independently — lands somewhere around $0.50–$3.00 in API spend depending on model tier. That's cheap against an analyst's hour, and expensive if you fire one off for every trivial lookup. Route by question difficulty, not by habit.

What to Actually Use

If you only pay for one thing, make it a deep-research agent — Gemini Deep Research if your work spans messy, regional, or PDF-heavy sources, OpenAI Deep Research if it's commercial and Western-web-centric. Pair it with Perplexity's free tier for the 80% of queries that are just fact lookups and don't deserve a 15-minute agent run. Add NotebookLM the moment you have a document set you actually own; it costs nothing and it's the only tool that structurally can't wander off into the open web and invent context. Researchers in the sciences should treat Elicit or Undermind as a separate, non-negotiable line item — general-purpose agents are not a substitute for real literature recall, and pretending otherwise is how systematic reviews get embarrassing. And whatever you choose: click the citations. The tools got dramatically better at finding sources this year. They did not get proportionally better at reading them.
