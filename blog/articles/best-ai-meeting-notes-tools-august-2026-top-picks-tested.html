The State of AI Meeting Notes in 2026

Five years ago, "AI meeting notes" meant a shaky transcript and a handful of misidentified speakers. In 2026, the category has split into two distinct camps: dedicated note-taking bots that join your call as a silent participant, and native AI companions built directly into Zoom, Teams, and Google Meet. Both camps have gotten dramatically better — word error rates that used to hover around 15-20% on accented or overlapping speech are now routinely under 6% on the leading tools — but they've also gotten more complicated to choose between, thanks to divergent pricing models, storage policies, and integration depth.

We spent three weeks running the same set of 14 meetings (sales calls, engineering standups, and one deliberately chaotic five-person brainstorm with two non-native English speakers) through seven of the most-used tools on the market. This roundup covers what actually held up.

What We Tested and How

Every tool was evaluated on the same criteria: transcription accuracy against a human-verified transcript, speaker diarization (correctly attributing who said what), summary quality (did it capture decisions and action items, not just topics), and how well it played with downstream tools like Slack, Notion, and CRM systems. We also tracked latency — how long after a call ends before a usable summary lands in your inbox.

Fathom: The Accuracy Leader

Fathom came out on top of our transcription accuracy tests, and it's the only tool in this roundup with a genuinely useful unlimited free tier — no meeting caps, no watermarks on exported notes. Its action-item extraction correctly flagged 22 of 24 real commitments made across our test meetings, missing only two that were phrased as soft suggestions rather than direct commitments ("maybe someone should look at the churn numbers" didn't get picked up, which is arguably correct behavior). The paid tier adds CRM sync (Salesforce, HubSpot) and team-wide searchable call libraries.

Fireflies.ai: Best for Integration Sprawl

If your team lives across a dozen tools, Fireflies has the widest integration net — over 40 apps including Asana, Jira, Notion, and Zapier out of the box. Its "AI Apps" feature lets you run custom summary templates per meeting type, which was genuinely useful for separating sales-call summaries from internal standups without manual reformatting. The tradeoff is a noticeably busier interface than Fathom or Granola.

Granola: Built for People Who Take Their Own Notes Too

Granola's pitch is different from the rest: instead of a bot joining your call, it runs locally and merges your own typed notes with the transcript afterward. For people who already jot rough notes during a meeting, this produced noticeably more useful summaries in our tests — your own emphasis and shorthand get folded into the structured output rather than discarded. It doesn't work for calls you're not personally attending, which is a real limitation for managers who want notes on meetings they skip.

Native Options: Zoom and Microsoft Teams

Zoom AI Companion and Microsoft Copilot for Teams are the "good enough, already paid for" options. Neither matched the dedicated tools on transcription accuracy in our tests, and Copilot's meeting features are gated behind a $30/user/month add-on on top of an existing Microsoft 365 E3/E5 license — expensive if meeting notes are the only Copilot feature you want. Zoom's version is more reasonably bundled but struggled the most with our accented-speaker test meeting, at an 11.3% error rate on that specific call versus 5.9% for Fathom on the same recording.

💡
Tip: Run a side-by-side test on one real meeting before committing to an annual plan. Accuracy varies more by accent, cross-talk, and audio quality than any published benchmark suggests — the tool that wins on a clean two-person call can fall apart on a five-person call with a bad conference mic.

Privacy and Data Retention

This is the part most reviews skip. Third-party bots like Fireflies, Otter, and Grain retain audio and transcripts on their own servers by default, and enterprise-grade data controls (SOC 2, on-request deletion, no-training guarantees) are usually locked behind the top pricing tier, not the plan most teams start on. Fathom and Granola both offer clearer default retention policies, and Granola's local-first architecture means less raw audio ever leaves your device. If you're recording calls with clients under an NDA, read the data processing addendum before rollout — not after.

💡
Legal and healthcare teams should specifically check whether a tool offers a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) or equivalent — most consumer-tier plans explicitly exclude regulated data from their terms of service.

Pricing Reality Check

Sticker prices are misleading because minute caps and seat minimums vary wildly. Fireflies' $18/month plan looks cheaper than Fathom's $19/month team plan until you account for Fathom's unlimited free individual tier — a five-person team might only need one or two paid seats for admin features, while Fireflies typically requires a paid seat per active user to unlock unlimited transcription minutes.

  • Solo users on a budget: Fathom's free tier is the most generous with zero meeting caps.
  • Sales teams needing CRM sync: Fireflies or Fathom's paid tier both handle Salesforce/HubSpot well.
  • Privacy-sensitive teams: Granola's local-first model reduces exposure the most.
  • Already paying for Microsoft 365 E5: Copilot may be worth it only if you're using other Copilot features too.

Our Recommendation

For most professionals and small teams, start with Fathom's free tier — it had the lowest error rate in our tests, imposes no meeting caps, and lets you evaluate real usage before paying anything. If your team needs deep CRM or project-management integrations, layer in Fireflies.ai once you've confirmed the workflow is worth automating. Skip the native Zoom or Teams AI add-ons unless you're already paying for the surrounding enterprise suite for other reasons — on accuracy and cost per meeting, both lag the dedicated tools we tested, and the Teams Copilot add-on in particular is hard to justify on meeting notes alone.
