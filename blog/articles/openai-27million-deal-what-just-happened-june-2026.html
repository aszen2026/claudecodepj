What Happened

The most expensive fight in AI politics this cycle wasn't over a product, a model release, or a regulation. It was over one person: Alex Bores, the New York State Assembly member who wrote the RAISE Act and is now running for U.S. Congress. As of today, June 26, 2026, the roughly $27 million proxy war fought around his candidacy has, per reporting, ended in a draw.

Here's the short version of how it unfolded:

  • The bill: Bores authored the RAISE Act, a New York state measure aimed at putting safety and transparency obligations on frontier AI developers. That made him a marked man in Silicon Valley's pro-acceleration wing.
  • The target: When Bores launched a congressional bid, he became the first real test case for AI's new political machinery — a chance to show whether the industry could end a regulator-friendly career early.
  • The money: Leading the Future, the pro-AI super PAC backed by venture firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) and OpenAI president Greg Brockman, was positioned to spend heavily against him. Counter-spending aligned with Anthropic's more regulation-tolerant posture lined up on the other side.
  • The outcome: After the dust settled on roughly $27 million in combined firepower, neither side landed a clean win. The pro-AI PAC's threatened blitz did not deliver a knockout, and Bores was not driven from the race.
💡
The number that matters: $27 million is enormous for a single House primary, but trivial next to the capital expenditure these same companies report each quarter. The spend signals intent — testing political muscle — more than it reflects financial strain.

Why It Matters

This was never really about Alex Bores. It was a stress test of whether the AI industry's largest players can convert market dominance into political dominance — and the answer, for now, is "not cleanly."

Who comes out ahead: Bores himself, mostly. Surviving a multimillion-dollar effort built partly to unseat you is its own kind of credential, and it hands AI-safety advocates a talking point: industry money is not yet decisive at the ballot box. Anthropic, by association, gets to look like the lab that didn't try to crush a sitting legislator.

Who has to recalibrate: a16z and the OpenAI-aligned camp behind Leading the Future. They demonstrated they can assemble serious money fast, but a draw against a single state assemblyman is not the deterrent effect a super PAC is built to project. Other lawmakers weighing AI rules just learned that the threat is survivable.

The broader signal for the industry: the fight has moved from comment letters and lobbyists to candidate-level electoral spending. That escalation is the real news, independent of who "won."

Context

This clash didn't appear from nowhere. It's the political tail of a regulatory fight that's been building since California's SB 1047 battle, where the same fault line — ship-fast accelerationists versus safety-first regulators — first drew industry money into open conflict. The RAISE Act is, in effect, New York's successor attempt, and Bores is its author.

It also reflects a genuine split inside the AI industry itself, not a unified front against regulation:

  • The pro-acceleration camp — a16z and figures like Greg Brockman — treats binding state-level AI rules as an existential drag on U.S. competitiveness and is now willing to fund campaigns to fight them.
  • Anthropic has spent the past two years publicly arguing that some guardrails are compatible with rapid progress, a stance that repeatedly puts it on the opposite side of OpenAI and its venture backers on policy.

That this disagreement is now playing out through opposing super PACs — rather than dueling blog posts — is the inflection point. A "draw" is the natural first result when two well-funded sides discover their money roughly cancels out.

💡
Watch the precedent, not the scoreboard. The lasting takeaway isn't the result in one race — it's that frontier labs and their investors have normalized direct electoral spending on AI policy. That template gets reused in 2026 and 2028.

What To Do Next

If you build, buy, or regulate AI, this episode is a planning input, not just a news item.

  • For developers and AI teams: Don't assume the pro-acceleration camp will keep regulation off the books. State-level rules like the RAISE Act survived a $27 million test. Treat model documentation, evaluation logs, and transparency reporting as near-term compliance work, not optional polish.
  • For enterprise buyers: Factor vendor political posture into procurement. A provider fighting safety legislation and one supporting it carry different regulatory and reputational risk profiles. Ask your shortlist — including OpenAI and Anthropic — where they stand on state AI rules, in writing.
  • For policy and government-affairs staff: The lesson for legislators is that industry money is now survivable, but only with organized counter-support. If you back AI rules, line up your coalition before you introduce the bill, not after the super PAC files.
  • For everyone watching the space: Track where Leading the Future and its Anthropic-aligned counterparts spend next. The targets they choose will tell you which regulations the industry fears most — a far better signal than any press release.

A draw resolves nothing on the merits. It just confirms the AI policy fight now has a campaign-finance front, and that front is open for the rest of the cycle.
