What Happened

Apple has sued OpenAI — and the complaint is not about patents, model weights, or the ChatGPT integration that Apple itself shipped into iOS. It is about people, and about what Apple says those people said on their way out the door.

The core of the filing is a trade-secrets claim built around recruiting. According to Apple, when its employees interviewed for jobs at OpenAI, the interviews functioned less like hiring conversations and more like debriefs: candidates were pressed on how Apple builds what it builds. That is the allegation that gives the suit its teeth, and it is why the most eye-catching passages in the complaint read like transcripts rather than technical exhibits.

A few things are worth separating carefully, because they will get blurred in the coverage this week:

  • This is a civil trade-secrets and poaching action, not an antitrust case and not an IP-infringement case about model training data.
  • Apple and OpenAI are still commercial partners. ChatGPT has been an opt-in fallback inside Siri since Apple announced the integration at WWDC in June 2024 and shipped it in the iOS 18 cycle. Nothing in the lawsuit, as reported, terminates that.
  • The alleged conduct is about the interview room — the flow of Apple's internal know-how out through candidates — rather than about code or files walking out on a hard drive.

Everything beyond that — damages sought, which specific programs Apple says were compromised, how OpenAI will answer — belongs to the filing and to OpenAI's forthcoming response, not to speculation.

💡
Read the complaint, not the headlines. "Wildest claims" lists are selected for quotability, not legal weight. In trade-secrets litigation, the claims that actually survive a motion to dismiss are usually the boring ones: a specifically identified secret, a duty to keep it, and a plausible route by which it moved. Quotable anecdotes about interview questions are colorful, but they are evidence of a route — not the secret itself.

Why It Matters

The immediate consequence lands on engineers, not on companies. Apple is asserting that the act of interviewing at a rival can itself be the vector of misappropriation. If that theory gets any traction in court, every AI lab's recruiting process becomes a legal surface area. Expect interview scripts to be lawyered, expect "tell me how you solved this at your last job" to be replaced by hypotheticals on synthetic problems, and expect candidates from major labs to be handed a warning sheet before their first onsite.

Who is exposed: the mid-career AI infrastructure engineer, who is the single most poached asset class in the industry right now. A live lawsuit that treats their interview answers as potential exfiltration makes them harder to hire and slower to move — which is, functionally, a chilling effect on labor mobility regardless of how the case resolves.

Who benefits: Apple, on both boards. If it wins, it gets an injunction-shaped moat around its Siri and on-device model work. If it merely fights, it raises the cost and the friction of recruiting out of Cupertino, which is a real defensive win for a company that has been visibly losing AI staff.

Who carries the awkwardness: OpenAI, which now has to litigate against a distribution partner whose devices put ChatGPT in front of hundreds of millions of users. Partner-versus-litigant is not a new posture in tech — Apple and Samsung sued each other for years while Apple bought Samsung displays — but it constrains how loudly either side can escalate.

Context

This is what a strategy under pressure looks like from the outside. Apple's AI position has been the same for two years: ship narrow, private, on-device features under the Apple Intelligence banner, rent a frontier model from someone else for the hard questions, and rebuild Siri on a longer timeline than anyone wanted. The rented-frontier-model part is OpenAI. The rebuild-Siri part is what the departing engineers know about.

Set that against the market. Meta spent 2025 running an openly cash-driven raid on rival research staff. Google folded DeepMind's model work directly into its consumer surface. OpenAI grew from a lab into a product company with a consumer-scale hiring appetite. In that environment, the scarce input is not compute and it is not data — it is the few thousand people who have shipped a large model into a shipping product. Apple is now using the courts to defend a resource it has struggled to defend with compensation.

The precedent hovering over all of this is the wave of trade-secrets suits that followed the self-driving talent wars — the same pattern of a big incumbent, a fast-moving rival, and a hiring pipeline reframed as a leak. Those cases were slow, expensive, and mostly settled. This one likely will be too.

💡
The signal to watch is not the verdict — it's the partnership. If ChatGPT quietly loses its privileged slot in Siri's fallback chain, or Apple announces a second frontier partner, that tells you more about the real state of Apple–OpenAI relations than any court filing will.

What To Do Next

If you are an engineer at a frontier lab or at Apple: assume interviews are now discoverable. Talk about what you can do, not about how your current employer does it — describe your skills using public problems and your own side work. Re-read your employment agreement's confidentiality and non-solicit clauses before your next onsite. This costs you nothing and removes you from the blast radius.

If you run recruiting or engineering hiring: audit your interview loops now. Any question that asks a candidate to describe a current employer's internal architecture, benchmarks, or roadmap is a liability with no compensating benefit — you can evaluate the same signal with a synthetic problem. Document the change.

If you build on Apple's or OpenAI's platforms: do nothing yet, but hedge structurally. The suit does not touch the ChatGPT-in-Siri integration or any developer API today. It does raise the odds that the two companies' roadmaps drift apart. Keep your model calls behind an abstraction layer so that swapping providers is a config change, not a rewrite — that is good practice regardless, and this is a concrete reason to actually do it.

If you are a user: nothing changes on your phone. Siri's ChatGPT fallback is still opt-in, still asks before sending a query, and is unaffected by the filing.
