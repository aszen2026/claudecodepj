What Happened

Universal Music Group, Warner Music Group and Sony Music Entertainment have finalized licensing agreements with Suno Inc. and Udio, the two AI music-generation startups the labels sued for copyright infringement in June 2024. The deals, which began rolling out to users this week, replace litigation with a licensing structure: both platforms now train on and reproduce elements of major-label catalogs under royalty terms, while individual artists get an opt-in dashboard controlling whether their voice, vocal style, or catalog can be used to generate new tracks.

The original lawsuits, filed on behalf of the three majors in the Southern District of New York and the Middle District of Tennessee, sought statutory damages of up to $150,000 per infringed recording — a figure that, multiplied across the hundreds of thousands of tracks the labels alleged were used without permission, put both companies' survival in question. Suno, backed by Lightspeed Venture Partners after a $125 million Series B in 2024, and Udio, founded by former Google DeepMind researchers and backed by Andreessen Horowitz, had spent two years generating songs while the underlying rights dispute remained unresolved.

Both platforms are now shipping "licensed" product tiers: subscription plans that bundle catalog-cleared stems and voice models, with royalty pass-through to rightsholders built into the pricing. Artists who opt out are blocked from being mimicked; those who opt in receive a negotiated revenue share, similar in structure to mechanical royalties on cover songs.

💡
For working musicians: both Suno and Udio have opened artist verification portals this month. If you're signed to UMG, WMG, or Sony, check whether your voice model status defaults to opt-in — several artists reported during the rollout that it did, requiring an active opt-out request.

Why It Matters

This ends the adversarial phase of the biggest legal fight in generative AI's history outside of the image-generation suits against Stability AI and Midjourney. Instead of AI music platforms operating in a legal gray zone, the majors have effectively chosen to become suppliers and revenue partners rather than plaintiffs — a template that mirrors how the labels eventually monetized sampling and remix culture rather than suing it out of existence.

The clearest winners are the major labels themselves, which gain a new royalty stream without having to build generative products in-house, and Suno and Udio, which shed years of legal overhang that had scared off enterprise partners and slowed institutional fundraising. A cleared rights chain also opens the door to placements in advertising, gaming, and film — markets that have avoided unlicensed AI music because of takedown risk.

The losers are less obvious but real. Independent labels and artists outside Merlin or similar aggregators aren't covered by these three deals and remain in the same unresolved legal position as before. Songwriters and music publishers are a separate fight entirely — the current agreements cover master recordings, not composition and publishing rights, meaning organizations like the National Music Publishers' Association still have open claims. And AI music tools that never pursued licensing deals now face a widening gap: they're cheaper to use but increasingly excluded from commercial placements that require clean rights.

Context

This settlement fits a pattern that's been building since 2023. YouTube's Dream Track experiment paid a small group of artists directly for the use of their voice in AI-generated snippets, testing the licensing model at small scale before this week's much larger deals. Deezer has spent the past year flagging AI-generated uploads — reporting in 2025 that roughly a fifth of daily track uploads to its platform were fully AI-generated — pushing streaming services toward mandatory disclosure labels rather than outright bans. Spotify has been moving in the same direction, testing "AI-made" tags on tracks rather than removing them.

What's different about the Suno and Udio deals is scale and source: this is the underlying training-data dispute being resolved directly with rightsholders, not a downstream distribution policy. It puts music ahead of most other creative industries in resolving the "was this trained on stolen work" question through commercial settlement rather than years of unresolved litigation — a path visual AI companies like Stability AI and Midjourney have not yet reached with major stock-image and publishing plaintiffs.

What To Do Next

  • Musicians and rightsholders: log into Suno's and Udio's artist portals to confirm your opt-in/opt-out status and review the royalty terms before assuming your consent status defaults the way you'd expect.
  • Developers building on generative audio APIs: budget for royalty pass-through costs on the newly licensed tiers, and confirm whether your existing integration is grandfathered into the unlicensed model or requires migration.
  • Agencies, game studios, and other commercial buyers: licensed AI tracks now carry a materially lower takedown and litigation risk than six months ago, but confirm the scope of the license — sync and broadcast rights are not automatically included in every consumer subscription tier.
  • Independent labels and publishers watching from outside these deals: expect this settlement structure, not further scorched-earth litigation, to become the negotiating template you'll be offered next.
💡
For product teams: if you shipped a feature on Suno's or Udio's unlicensed API, check your terms of service update — several integration partners have a 60-to-90-day window to migrate to the licensed tier before rate limits or feature access change.
