What Happened

Howard Lutnick, the former Cantor Fitzgerald chairman who became U.S. Secretary of Commerce in early 2025, has spent the past year turning the Commerce Department into the single most important regulator of the AI hardware supply chain. His lever is the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), the sub-agency that decides which advanced chips can be exported and to whom.

The signature move came in August 2025, when the administration struck an unusual arrangement with Nvidia and AMD: the companies would be allowed to resume selling China-specific AI accelerators — Nvidia's H20 and AMD's MI308 — in exchange for handing the U.S. government 15% of the revenue from those sales. Lutnick publicly championed the terms, framing export licenses as something companies pay for rather than receive by right.

In parallel, Commerce has been reworking the CHIPS Act. Instead of disbursing grants as originally structured, Lutnick pushed to convert federal support into equity — most visibly the government taking a roughly 10% stake in Intel. He has also kept Section 232 semiconductor tariffs on the table as leverage over where chipmakers build fabs.

Why It Matters

For the AI industry, Commerce is no longer a background agency — it is a gatekeeper that sits between model builders and the compute they need.

  • Chipmakers win access, lose margin. Nvidia and AMD get back a multibillion-dollar China market, but a 15% cut off the top and unpredictable licensing turns export policy into a running tax on their most geopolitically exposed SKUs.
  • Chinese AI labs get a conditional lifeline. H20-class parts are deliberately throttled below flagship performance, so buyers like ByteDance and Alibaba can train and serve models — just not at the frontier, and only while Washington keeps the door open.
  • U.S. hyperscalers get relative advantage. Every constraint on China-bound silicon widens the compute gap that OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta already enjoy on domestic-grade Blackwell and MI300-series hardware.
  • Taxpayers become shareholders. Equity stakes and revenue shares tie government finances to the commercial success of specific companies — a structural change with few precedents in tech policy.
💡
The real signal: when export licensing carries a price tag, "can we ship this chip?" becomes a quarterly financial variable, not a one-time legal question. Treat BIS decisions as a line item, not a footnote.

Context

This didn't start with Lutnick. The Biden-era Commerce Department built the original tiered export-control regime — the October 2022 rules, the 2023 tightening that first forced Nvidia to design the H20, and the 2025 "AI diffusion" framework that sorted countries into access tiers. Lutnick inherited that machinery and made it transactional.

The shift mirrors the administration's broader posture: treat market access as a negotiable asset. The same logic that produced tariff threats against trading partners now shapes who can buy an accelerator. It also rhymes with moves elsewhere in the stack — pressure on TSMC and Samsung over U.S. fab commitments, and scrutiny of where model training compute physically lives.

For competitors watching, the takeaway is that the frontier is being defended at the hardware layer, not the model layer. You can open-weight a model; you cannot open-weight a fab or a license.

What To Do Next

Concrete steps depending on where you sit:

  • If you buy compute: Diversify supply assumptions. Don't build capacity plans that depend on a single vendor's China-tier part remaining licensable through 2027. Price in the 15% revenue share if you're reselling China-region inference.
  • If you build models: Confirm the export status of the exact SKU behind your cloud region, not just the vendor. An "H100 equivalent" in one geography may be an H20 in another, with materially different throughput.
  • If you're a startup founder: Assume compliance is now a product requirement. Know your chips' provenance well enough to answer an enterprise procurement questionnaire — buyers are starting to ask.
  • If you're an investor or analyst: Read BIS rule changes and Commerce statements as earnings-relevant. A licensing tweak can move a chipmaker's China guidance more than a product launch.
💡
Watch the paper, not the podium. Lutnick's public comments set direction, but the binding details live in Federal Register entries and BIS license conditions. Bookmark the Commerce/BIS rules page and check it before making a compute commitment tied to a restricted market.
