What Happened

The Financial Times has spent the past two years turning itself into one of the most aggressive test cases for how a legacy news organization should deal with generative AI — both as a supplier of training data and as a builder of AI products. Two moves anchor the story.

  • April 29, 2024: The FT and OpenAI announced a strategic licensing and product partnership. Under the deal, FT journalism became available inside ChatGPT with attribution and links back to ft.com, and the FT gained access to OpenAI technology to develop its own AI features. Financial terms were not disclosed.
  • 2025: The FT rolled out "Ask FT," a generative-AI search and answer tool for subscribers, built on Anthropic's Claude. It lets paying readers query two decades of FT reporting in natural language and get sourced, citation-linked answers rather than a list of headlines.

The pattern is deliberate: license the archive to a frontier-model lab for distribution and revenue, then use a (different) frontier model to build a retrieval product the FT controls. The FT licensed content to OpenAI while building its flagship reader tool on Claude — a multi-vendor posture, not an exclusive bet.

Why It Matters

This is the clearest working example of a publisher trying to be both a data partner and a product company at once, and the winners and losers fall out along predictable lines.

  • Winners — premium, paywalled publishers. The FT's value is a deep, structured, high-trust archive. That is exactly what model labs will pay for and what readers will pay to query. Outlets with a similar profile (Reuters, Bloomberg, The Economist) have the same leverage.
  • Winners — the model labs. OpenAI gets licensed, attributable, lawsuit-proof data; Anthropic gets a marquee media reference customer for Claude.
  • Losers — ad-funded, commodity-news sites. If answers are synthesized inside ChatGPT or an on-site assistant, the click — and the ad impression — never happens. Publishers without a subscription moat or a distinctive archive have little to license and little to defend.
💡
The real signal: the FT split its vendors on purpose. Licensing to OpenAI while building on Claude keeps it from being captured by any single lab's pricing or roadmap. Treat single-vendor AI dependence as a strategic risk, not a convenience.

Context

The FT's approach sits between the two poles the rest of the industry has staked out.

  • The litigation pole: The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023, alleging mass copyright infringement. That case framed AI training as theft to be fought in court.
  • The licensing pole: A wave of deals went the other way. News Corp signed with OpenAI in May 2024 in an arrangement reported at up to $250 million over five years. Axel Springer, the Associated Press, Le Monde, Vox Media, The Atlantic, and Dotdash Meredith struck their own agreements.

The FT planted a flag on the licensing side early — but added the part most publishers skipped: shipping its own consumer-facing AI product instead of only renting its archive out. That combination, license-and-build, is now the template others are copying. The open question the industry still hasn't answered is whether licensing fees and retention gains from tools like "Ask FT" can offset the referral traffic that AI answer-engines quietly erode.

What To Do Next

Concrete steps depending on where you sit:

  • If you run a publication: Audit what you actually own — a deep, unique, well-structured archive is the only thing worth licensing. If you don't have one, a licensing deal won't save you; a defensible product or subscription will. Negotiate attribution and click-through into any AI deal, not just a flat fee.
  • If you're a developer building on news content: Don't scrape. The licensed-corpus era is here, and labs are paying for clean, attributable data precisely to avoid the NYT-style legal exposure. Build retrieval-augmented features on licensed sources with visible citations — that is what "Ask FT" demonstrates works.
  • If you're a buyer evaluating an AI content product: Ask two questions — which model powers it and is the vendor single-sourced? The FT's split between OpenAI (distribution) and Anthropic (product) shows that the smart play is keeping options open across labs.
  • If you're a reader: Tools like "Ask FT" are subscriber perks, not free utilities. The trade is explicit: you pay, you get a sourced answer engine over trusted reporting. Decide whether that's worth more to you than a general-purpose chatbot's unsourced summary.
💡
For teams: Before licensing your content or building on someone else's, write down what happens to your referral traffic if AI answers replace the click. If you can't model that number, you're negotiating blind.
