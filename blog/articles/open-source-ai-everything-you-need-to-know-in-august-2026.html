Open Weights Have Closed the Gap

Eighteen months ago, "open source AI" mostly meant a slightly worse, slightly cheaper version of GPT-4. That's no longer true. By mid-2026, models like DeepSeek-V3.2, Llama 4 Maverick, and Qwen 3 are matching or beating closed frontier models on coding and reasoning benchmarks, while running on hardware a serious hobbyist can actually afford. The catch is that "open source" now spans a messy spectrum from genuinely permissive Apache 2.0 releases to community licenses with revenue caps and usage restrictions that surprise people at the worst possible time — usually right after they've shipped a product on top of one.

This isn't a "top 10 AI tools" listicle. It's a working breakdown of which open-weight models are actually worth your GPU hours in 2026, what they're good at, and where the licensing fine print will bite you.

The Models That Matter Right Now

DeepSeek-V3.2 and R2

DeepSeek remains the price-performance outlier. V3.2 is a 671B-parameter mixture-of-experts model (37B active per token) that scores 89.1 on MMLU-Pro and 82% on LiveCodeBench, putting it within striking distance of GPT-5-class closed models on coding tasks. The R2 reasoning variant adds extended chain-of-thought and holds up well on AIME-style math benchmarks. Training cost was reported at under $6M, which is why every other lab has since scrambled to match its efficiency tricks. License is MIT — genuinely permissive, commercial use included.

Llama 4 (Scout and Maverick)

Meta's Llama 4 line moved to native multimodality and a mixture-of-experts architecture. Maverick (400B total, 17B active) handles long-context document analysis well — Meta claims a 1M-token context window, though in practice recall quality degrades noticeably past ~400K tokens in independent testing. The Llama Community License is not OSI-approved open source: companies with over 700 million monthly active users need a separate commercial agreement, and you can't use Llama outputs to train competing models. Fine for 99% of teams, worth reading for the other 1%.

Qwen 3

Alibaba's Qwen 3 family (from 0.6B up to a 235B MoE flagship) has become the default choice for multilingual work, particularly anything touching Chinese, Japanese, or Southeast Asian languages where Llama and Mistral historically underperform. Apache 2.0 licensed across the board. The 32B dense variant is a sweet spot for teams that want strong instruction-following without MoE routing complexity in production.

Mistral Large 3 and Mixtral

Mistral's newer releases split into two license tracks: Mistral Large 3 ships under the Mistral Research License (non-commercial without a paid agreement), while the smaller Ministral and Mixtral lines stay Apache 2.0. Mixtral 8x22B is still a solid workhorse for teams that want EU-hosted inference options and don't need frontier-level reasoning.

Gemma 3

Google's Gemma 3 (up to 27B) isn't trying to compete at the frontier — it's optimized for single-GPU and edge deployment, with strong function-calling reliability that punches above its parameter count. Custom license, commercial use allowed, but redistribution terms are stricter than Apache 2.0.

💡
Don't confuse "open weights" with "open source." Only a handful of these models (DeepSeek, Qwen, most Mistral small models) ship under true OSI licenses like Apache 2.0 or MIT. The rest are "open weight, restricted license" — you can download and run them, but redistribution, competitive use, or scale thresholds may require a commercial agreement. Read the actual license file, not the marketing page.

Running These Models Without a Data Center

Quantization has made most of this list accessible on consumer hardware. A 4-bit GGUF quant of Qwen 3-32B or Gemma 3-27B runs comfortably on a single RTX 4090 (24GB) via Ollama or llama.cpp, with throughput around 25-35 tokens/sec depending on context length. The 400B+ MoE models (DeepSeek, Llama 4 Maverick) need either a multi-GPU workstation or a rented cloud instance — expect to pay $2-4/hour for the GPU capacity to run DeepSeek-V3.2 at usable speed, which is still far cheaper than most closed-model API bills at production volume.

💡
If you're prototyping and don't need to self-host, providers like Together AI, Fireworks, and DeepInfra serve these open-weight models as hosted APIs at a fraction of GPT-5 or Claude pricing — often 5-10x cheaper per token — which is a good way to test a model's fit before committing GPU budget to self-hosting it.

The Licensing Trap Nobody Warns You About

  • Revenue and user thresholds: Llama's 700M MAU cap only affects a handful of companies, but similar clauses in smaller vendors' licenses can trip up growing startups faster than expected.
  • Output ownership clauses: Some research licenses (Mistral Large 3 included) restrict using model outputs to train or fine-tune competing models — relevant if you're generating synthetic training data.
  • Redistribution vs. hosting: Running a model behind your own API is usually fine under restrictive licenses; redistributing the weights themselves often is not.
  • License drift between versions: A model family's license can change between major releases (Mistral moved from fully open to a mixed model). Pin your license review to the specific version you're deploying, not the brand name.

Which One Should You Actually Use

For most teams building products, start with Qwen 3-32B or Mixtral 8x22B — both Apache 2.0, both cheap to self-host, and both good enough for the majority of real-world tasks without licensing risk. If your workload is coding-heavy and you have the infrastructure for a larger MoE model, DeepSeek-V3.2 currently delivers the best reasoning-per-dollar of anything in this category, open or closed. Reach for Llama 4 or Gemma 3 specifically when you need Meta's or Google's tooling ecosystem (fine-tuning pipelines, on-device deployment kits) rather than for raw benchmark performance — and in every case, have someone actually read the license before it ships to production, not after.
