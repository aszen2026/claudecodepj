What Happened

The Financial Times sits at the center of one of the most closely watched experiments in publishing: turning a 137-year-old newsroom into both a supplier of training data to AI labs and a builder of its own generative tools. Two threads define the current moment.

  • The licensing side. In April 2024, the FT signed a content-licensing and product-development partnership with OpenAI, allowing ChatGPT to surface attributed FT journalism with links back to the source. The deal made the FT one of the first major financial publishers to formally license its archive rather than litigate.
  • The product side. The FT built "Ask FT," a generative search assistant that lets subscribers query two decades of FT reporting in natural language and get synthesized, cited answers. Notably, the FT built it on Anthropic's Claude models — a deliberate separation between the lab it licenses content to and the lab it builds products with.

The FT, owned by Japan's Nikkei since 2015, has consistently said any AI use must be accuracy-first and reporter-supervised, with no automated publishing of AI-generated articles without human oversight.

💡
The key tension to watch: the FT is simultaneously a data seller (to OpenAI) and a tool builder (on Claude). That dual posture is becoming the template for premium publishers who refuse to pick a single AI partner.

Why It Matters

For an AI industry audience, the FT's moves matter because high-quality, structured, paywalled financial journalism is exactly the data that general-purpose models are starving for. The open web is increasingly polluted with synthetic text; verified, dated, editorially-checked reporting is scarce and valuable.

  • Winners: Labs that secure licensed corpora gain a defensible quality and citation advantage over competitors scraping degraded public data. Publishers with deep archives and trusted brands gain a new revenue line and legal cover.
  • Losers: Smaller publishers without leverage to negotiate face the same scraping pressure with none of the upside. And any lab betting purely on unlicensed scraping is now exposed to the litigation risk the FT chose to sidestep.

The product angle matters too: "Ask FT" shows that publishers no longer have to choose between blocking AI and being commoditized by it. They can ship retrieval-augmented tools on top of their own paywall, keeping users — and the subscription relationship — inside their walls.

Context

The FT's strategy only makes sense against the wider 2023–2025 realignment between newsrooms and AI labs:

  • Axel Springer × OpenAI (Dec 2023) — the first big European publisher deal, surfacing Politico and Business Insider content in ChatGPT.
  • The New York Times × OpenAI/Microsoft lawsuit (filed Dec 2023) — the litigation path, alleging mass copyright infringement and still shaping the legal baseline for "fair use" in model training.
  • News Corp × OpenAI (May 2024) — reported at roughly $250 million over five years, the largest publicly disclosed publisher deal and a benchmark for what premium financial and business content can command.

The FT chose the licensing path, not the courtroom — and then hedged by building product on a different model provider. That "license to one, build on another" posture is the genuinely instructive move. It treats AI labs as interchangeable suppliers rather than exclusive partners, preserving negotiating leverage as the market matures and as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over proprietary archives becomes the dominant publisher pattern.

💡
If you're benchmarking AI-publisher economics, the News Corp deal's ~$50M/year is the number to anchor against. Premium financial archives appear to clear at a meaningful multiple over general news.

What To Do Next

For developers building on licensed content: Don't assume scraped data is safe to ship. Prefer licensed corpora or first-party data for anything customer-facing, and design your RAG pipeline to preserve source attribution and links from day one — both OpenAI's FT integration and "Ask FT" treat citation as a hard requirement, not a nicety.

For publishers and content owners: Inventory your archive before negotiating. The FT's leverage came from depth, structure, and brand trust. Decide explicitly whether you are a data seller, a tool builder, or both — and avoid signing exclusivity that locks you to a single lab.

For buyers and enterprise teams: If you depend on financial or market intelligence, evaluate publisher-native assistants like "Ask FT" against general chatbots. The trade-off is narrower coverage but verifiable, dated, attributable answers — which for compliance-sensitive financial work often outweighs raw breadth.

For everyone: Treat any AI answer drawn from news as only as trustworthy as its citation. The publishers worth following are the ones making the source visible — not the ones hiding it.
