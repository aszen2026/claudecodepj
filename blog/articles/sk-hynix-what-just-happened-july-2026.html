What Happened

SK Hynix shares pushed to fresh record highs on the KOSPI on July 29, 2026, after the company's latest quarterly results showed operating profit and revenue once again driven almost entirely by high-bandwidth memory (HBM) sales to Nvidia. The company has now strung together several consecutive record-setting quarters since HBM3E ramped in 2024, and the latest report confirms that HBM has moved from a niche, high-margin product line to the single biggest swing factor in SK Hynix's overall financial performance.

The headline development inside the earnings release is the acceleration of HBM4 — the next-generation memory standard that pairs with Nvidia's Rubin GPU platform — from qualification into volume shipments. SK Hynix has said it is prioritizing HBM4 output at its Cheongju M15X fab and continuing construction on the Yongin semiconductor cluster to keep pace with demand that, by the company's own account, remains sold out well into 2027.

💡
Quick context: HBM (high-bandwidth memory) is stacked DRAM that sits next to an AI accelerator's compute die, feeding it data far faster than conventional memory. It's now one of the most expensive and supply-constrained components in any AI GPU system — often a bigger bottleneck than the GPU die itself.

Alongside the earnings, SK Hynix reiterated it is raising capital expenditure guidance for 2026, funneling more of its budget toward HBM4 and HBM4E capacity rather than commodity DRAM or NAND, a shift that has been underway since the AI buildout began accelerating in 2023–2024.

Why It Matters

SK Hynix's results matter beyond one company's stock price because it sits at a structural chokepoint in the AI supply chain. Nvidia cannot ship Blackwell- or Rubin-class GPUs without qualified HBM, and SK Hynix has held the leading share of that market — reported at well over half of global HBM output — since it became Nvidia's first and largest HBM3E supplier. Every quarter SK Hynix posts record HBM revenue is effectively a read on how tight AI compute supply remains.

The winners are clear: SK Hynix captures outsized margins on a product few competitors can yet match at volume, and Nvidia locks in the memory it needs to keep GPU shipments on schedule. The pressure falls on Samsung Electronics, which has struggled through repeated qualification delays for its HBM3E and HBM4 parts with Nvidia, and on Micron, which has carved out a smaller but growing HBM3E/HBM4 position, largely by selling to Nvidia and hyperscaler customers that want a second source.

There's also a spillover effect for buyers of ordinary memory. As SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron redirect fab capacity and capex toward HBM, conventional DRAM and NAND supply tightens, which has already pushed up pricing for PC, smartphone, and server memory through 2025 and into 2026 — a cost that eventually reaches consumer electronics and enterprise IT budgets, not just AI data centers.

Context

SK Hynix's current position didn't happen overnight. The company bet early on HBM when it was still a specialty product for high-performance computing, and that bet paid off when Nvidia's H100 and H200 generations made HBM3/HBM3E a hard requirement rather than an option. Samsung, by contrast, has spent the better part of two years working to pass Nvidia's qualification bar, repeatedly pushing back timelines that once promised parity with SK Hynix.

This fits the broader arc of the 2024–2026 AI memory supercycle: as GPU compute has scaled, memory bandwidth has become the limiting factor, turning what used to be a commodity, cyclical memory business into one increasingly shaped by long-term supply agreements and pre-committed capacity — more akin to how foundry capacity gets allocated at TSMC than how DRAM pricing traditionally worked.

💡
Why HBM4 is the next inflection point: HBM4 requires a base logic die that's more tightly co-designed with the GPU maker, which is why Nvidia's qualification process has become a gating factor for suppliers. Whoever ramps HBM4 cleanly first captures the next multi-year contract cycle tied to Rubin and its successors.

What To Do Next

For different audiences, the practical takeaways from this report differ:

  • Investors should watch two things closely: Samsung's next HBM4 qualification update with Nvidia, and SK Hynix's actual capex spend versus guidance — a gap in either direction will move memory stocks more than the headline earnings beat.
  • AI infrastructure buyers and cloud teams should assume GPU lead times and pricing remain elevated through at least 2027, since HBM supply — not GPU die fabrication alone — is now the binding constraint; locking in capacity commitments early with Nvidia or its resellers is worth the negotiating effort.
  • Hardware OEMs and enterprise buyers of non-AI systems (laptops, servers, phones) should budget for continued conventional DRAM/NAND price pressure, since fab capacity is being reallocated toward HBM at the margin.
  • Competitors and component-strategy teams should track Micron's HBM4 roadmap as the more realistic near-term second source, given Samsung's slower qualification path, when building supplier diversification plans.

The next major checkpoint will be Samsung's own earnings and any Nvidia qualification news, followed by SK Hynix's commentary on HBM4E timing later in 2026 — both will indicate whether SK Hynix's lead widens further or narrows as rivals close the gap.
