A 24B-parameter model now runs on a $599 Mac mini and answers most coding questions before you finish reading them. That single fact has done more to push local AI into daily workflows than two years of "the cloud is too expensive" complaints. In early 2026, running a capable model on your own hardware stopped being a hobbyist stunt and became a defensible engineering choice — for privacy, for cost, and increasingly for raw latency.

This is a practical look at what actually works on a laptop or a single workstation today, what it costs, and where local still loses to the frontier.

Why local crossed the line in 2026

Three things changed at once. Quantization got good enough that a 4-bit model loses only a few points on most benchmarks instead of falling apart. Consumer hardware shipped with enough unified memory to hold a useful model — Apple's M4 Pro machines start at 24GB, and AMD's Strix Halo chips put 128GB of shared memory in a mini-PC. And the open-weight models themselves caught up to where GPT-4-class output felt routine.

The result: a Qwen3-32B or Llama-4-Scout running locally can draft a function, summarize a 40-page PDF that never leaves your disk, or power an autocomplete plugin with sub-200ms first-token latency. No API key, no per-token meter, no compliance review about where your customer data went.

💡
Rule of thumb for sizing: a model needs roughly its parameter count in billions, times the bytes-per-weight, as RAM. A 14B model at 4-bit quantization (~0.5 bytes/weight effective) needs about 9–10GB, leaving headroom on a 16GB machine. A 70B model at 4-bit wants 40GB+ — that's a 64GB workstation, not a laptop.

What the numbers really look like

Marketing says "runs on your laptop." Reality is more textured. Here's what you can expect, measured in tokens per second (higher is better for how fast text streams back):

  • M4 Max MacBook Pro (128GB): ~40 tok/s on a 70B 4-bit model, ~90 tok/s on a 14B. Genuinely usable for chat and code.
  • RTX 4090 (24GB): ~50 tok/s on a 32B model that fits in VRAM; spills badly and drops to single digits if you exceed it.
  • Mid-range laptop, CPU only (32GB, no discrete GPU): 5–9 tok/s on a 14B model. Tolerable for batch jobs, painful for interactive chat.

The cliff matters more than the peak. The moment a model doesn't fit in your fastest memory, throughput collapses by 5–10x. Choosing a model one size down that fits entirely in VRAM almost always beats a bigger model that spills.

💡
Quantization sweet spot: Q4_K_M (4-bit) is the default most people should use — near-full quality at roughly a quarter the memory. Drop to Q8 only if you have spare RAM and need the last 2–3% of accuracy; go below 4-bit only when you're desperate to fit, and expect noticeable degradation on reasoning tasks.

Where local still loses

Local AI is not a drop-in replacement for frontier models, and pretending otherwise wastes your time. A 32B local model handles 80% of everyday tasks — drafting, summarizing, simple code, classification — at quality you won't distinguish from a cloud call. The remaining 20% is where the gap is brutal.

  • Hard reasoning and long agentic chains: frontier models like Claude Opus 4.8 and the latest GPT release still pull clearly ahead on multi-step problems and large-context coding.
  • Context windows: running a model at 128K context locally eats memory fast and slows generation. Cloud providers absorb that cost; your laptop doesn't.
  • Maintenance: you own the updates, the prompt tuning, and the "why is it suddenly slow" debugging. There's no support ticket.

The cost math is where local wins decisively for heavy users. A developer running 2 million tokens a day through a cloud API can spend $300–800/month. The same workload on a $2,000 workstation pays for itself in two to six months, then runs effectively free. Light users — a few queries a day — will never recoup the hardware and should just use an API.

What to actually do

If you're a developer or professional who handles sensitive data, runs AI constantly, or works offline, start here: install Ollama, pull a 14B model like Qwen3-14B if you have 16GB of RAM or a 32B model if you have 32GB+, and point your existing tools at http://localhost:11434. Spend a week running your real workload through it. You'll quickly learn which tasks it nails and which ones still need a cloud call — and that split is exactly the information that tells you whether to invest in bigger hardware. For everyone else with occasional needs, a frontier API is still cheaper, smarter, and zero maintenance. Local AI in 2026 isn't about replacing the cloud everywhere; it's about owning the 80% of work that doesn't need to leave your machine.
