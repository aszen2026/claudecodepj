What Happened

Google DeepMind and A24 — the studio behind Everything Everywhere All at Once, Hereditary, and Uncut Gems — announced a $75 million partnership to build AI filmmaking tools for Hollywood. The deal pairs DeepMind's generative video research with A24's production pipeline, marking the first time a major AI lab has tied a nine-figure commitment to a prestige film studio rather than a streaming giant or a stock-footage library.

Here is what the headline and announcement establish:

  • Who: Google DeepMind (the research lab behind Gemini and the Veo video models) and A24, the independent studio and distributor.
  • How much: $75 million, structured as a joint build effort rather than a one-time licensing fee.
  • What: AI tools aimed at film production — the kind of work DeepMind's Veo line of text-to-video and image-to-video models targets.
  • When: Announced June 23, 2026.

The framing matters. This is not "A24 licenses a chatbot." It is two parties co-developing tooling, which means DeepMind gets a working studio as a testbed and A24 gets early, customized access to frontier video generation.

💡
Read the structure, not just the number. A $75M co-development deal signals DeepMind wants production feedback loops, not just a logo. Watch whether the output is internal tooling A24 keeps, or a product Google eventually sells to every studio.

Why It Matters

For two years the AI-in-Hollywood story has been adversarial: studios versus the people worried AI would replace them, capped by the 2023 WGA and SAG-AFTRA strikes that put AI guardrails into union contracts. A name-brand creative studio publicly spending real money to build with an AI lab reframes that fight. It says the tools have crossed from demo-reel curiosity into something a working studio will stake budget on.

Who wins:

  • Google DeepMind gets credibility its rivals can't buy off the shelf — a respected studio validating Veo against real production demands like shot continuity, character consistency, and director intent.
  • A24 gets a cost lever. Pre-visualization, concept art, VFX iteration, and reshoot planning are exactly the expensive, time-boxed tasks where generative video can compress weeks into hours.

Who's exposed:

  • VFX vendors and pre-viz shops whose bread-and-butter is the iteration A24 may now do in-house.
  • Crew and creative guilds, who will read $75M as a number that buys the very automation their contracts tried to fence off. Expect the consent, credit, and compensation questions to resurface fast.
  • OpenAI and Runway, who now have to answer why a marquee studio chose Google.

Context

This lands in a crowded race. OpenAI's Sora kicked off the text-to-video arms race, and Runway has spent the last two years embedding itself in production — its 2024 deal with Lionsgate to train a custom model on the studio's catalog was the first real "AI lab meets studio" pairing. DeepMind's Veo models have been the company's answer, pushing on longer clips, higher fidelity, and tighter prompt control.

What's different here is the partner profile. Lionsgate is a catalog-heavy commercial studio; the Runway deal was largely about licensing a back catalog to train on. A24 is a tastemaker known for directorial vision and tight budgets, not franchise volume. DeepMind picking A24 is a bet that the credibility of how films get made matters more than the size of the library you train on.

It also fits Google's broader pattern of buying its way into verticals it can't win on research alone — pairing frontier models with domain partners who supply the messy real-world constraints a benchmark never captures. Music had its label negotiations; film now has its $75M flagship.

💡
The benchmark that matters isn't FID or clip length. It's whether a director can get the same character, lighting, and tone across a sequence of shots. That's the production-grade problem A24's involvement is meant to pressure-test — and the one rivals' demos still dodge.

What To Do Next

If you're a filmmaker or studio operator:

  • Treat generative video as a pre-production and iteration tool first — storyboards, mood films, VFX previz — where mistakes are cheap and nothing ships to an audience yet.
  • Get your rights and consent terms in writing before you generate anything featuring a performer's likeness or voice. The union guardrails from the 2023 strikes still apply, deal or no deal.

If you're a developer building on these models:

  • Watch for whether DeepMind productizes what it builds with A24. If a "Veo for production" API or tier appears, the A24 collaboration is your spec sheet for what's actually supported.
  • Design around consistency and controllability, not raw generation. The differentiated work is in shot-to-shot coherence, editing handoff (think clean exports to existing NLE pipelines), and asset reuse.

If you're a buyer evaluating tools:

  • Don't pay for headline clip quality. Ask vendors to demo a multi-shot sequence with a fixed character and setting — the exact thing this partnership is built to solve and the exact thing most demos avoid.
  • Budget for the human-in-the-loop. Even at A24's scale, $75M buys tooling, not autonomy — the editing, direction, and judgment stay with people.
