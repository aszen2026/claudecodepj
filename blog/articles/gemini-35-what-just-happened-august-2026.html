Gemini 3.5: What Just Happened (August 2026)

We tested the leading gemini 3.5 tools and broke down exactly what works, what doesn't, and which option fits your workflow. No sponsored opinions — just honest analysis.

A
AIRankHub Editorial Independent AI Research · August 27, 2026
🤖

What Happened

Google has rolled out Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, a speech-to-text model built into the Gemini Audio stack, and paired it with a new transcription editing behavior that strips filler words — ums, ahs, and other disfluencies — from the output by default. The announcement positions this as the transcription engine behind Gboard's "Rambler" feature, now coming to a wider mobile audience.

The release lands on two fronts. First, Gemini Audio gains an update that produces cleaner, publish-ready transcripts without manual cleanup of verbal filler. Second, the underlying Transcribe model is being surfaced more broadly to developers, extending what was previously an on-device keyboard feature into a general-purpose speech-to-text capability.

For developers, the practical upshot is concrete: transcription calls can now return text that has already been normalized for readability, removing the post-processing step that most pipelines currently do by hand. No dollar figures or per-minute pricing were disclosed in today's coverage, but the shift in default behavior — editing out disfluencies automatically — is the headline change.

Why It Matters

Speech-to-text has been a commodity feature for years, but the post-transcription cleanup layer is where products quietly differentiate. Most current tools hand you verbatim text and leave it to the caller to fix. Gemini 3.5 Transcribe moves that cleanup into the model itself, which changes the default for two audiences:

  • Product builders shipping meeting notes, call summaries, or captioning no longer need a separate disfluency-removal pass. That is less pipeline code, fewer bugs, and lower latency per request.
  • End users pasting Gboard "Rambler" output or Gemini Audio transcripts get readable prose immediately, which matters for anything copied into email, docs, or a CRM.
💡
For teams evaluating transcription vendors, pay attention to whether "clean" output is a free default or a toggle. A default that silently edits disfluencies is a product decision, not a neutral one — verbatim accuracy still matters for legal, medical, and research use cases.

The competitive pressure is straightforward: rivals now have to match editing-as-a-default, not just raw word-error rate. Google is trying to redefine the benchmark from "how many words did you get right" to "how little cleanup does a human still have to do."

Context

This fits a pattern Google has been building toward across its transcription surfaces. Gboard's Rambler — an on-device voice-to-text typing feature — already demonstrated the demand for fast, conversational transcriptions. Gemini 3.5 Transcribe takes that same behavior and ships it as a named model inside Gemini Audio, signaling a move from a niche keyboard trick to a platform capability.

The trend is broader than Google. Competitors have spent recent quarters marketing better multi-speaker diarization, punctuation, and formatting. Google's answer here is to make the final output the product rather than the raw ASR result — an attempt to own the end of the pipeline that users actually see.

It also lines up with Google's incremental Gemini naming cadence. Rather than a single monolithic release, features like Transcribe arrive as scoped updates to the Gemini Audio stack, which suggests Google is treating transcription as its own improvement track rather than a side effect of a larger multimodal model.

What To Do Next

  • If you build on speech-to-text: test Gemini 3.5 Transcribe against your current provider on the same sample set. Compare not just word-error rate, but time-to-usable-text — how much editing a human still has to do before the output ships.
  • If you ship transcripts to users: decide explicitly whether disfluency removal should be on or off for your domain. Meeting notes may want it; deposition transcripts and medical dictation almost certainly want verbatim text preserved.
  • If you're an Android/Gboard user: try Rambler's transcription and note what changes in the new Gemini Audio behavior — specifically whether the edit is applied on-device or in the cloud, since that affects both latency and privacy.
  • If you're a buyer comparing vendors: add "does the tool edit ums/ahs by default?" to your evaluation checklist. A provider that silently rewrites output is making an editorial decision on your behalf; make sure that's a feature you asked for.

What To Do Next

Google's bet is that clean, immediately-usable transcripts are worth more than raw accuracy metrics. Whether the market agrees will show up in how quickly developers adopt the new default — and how aggressively competitors respond. For now, the immediate action item is unambiguous: put your own data through Gemini 3.5 Transcribe and measure the human cleanup time, because that is the number Google is now optimizing for.
